**Marinova, Dani M., [de Carvalho, Gabriela](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=4N5F0gIAAAAJ&hl=en) & [González Motos, Sheila](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=RCn34NAAAAAJ&hl=en) "Do equal, non-transferable parental leave rights change employer behavior? A post-reform correspondence test on job discrimination in Spain." [Pre-registration](https://osf.io/mre6p).**

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
Spain introduced equal non-transferable parental leave entitlements for men and women to reduce gender-based discrimination. We test the impact of the reform with a correspondence experiment. Five family configurations vary candidates’ caregiving burden and anticipated parental leave, partly replicating a 2016 pre-reform correspondence experiment as our baseline. Results indicate that candidates who signal imminent fertility are called back at the same rate as candidates signaling none, for both genders. Penalties attach instead to caregiving burden and are gendered: single mothers fare worst, receiving roughly half the callbacks of single fathers. The same pattern governs the order in which employers make contact where fathers retain the small and statistically insignificant advantage reported for before the reform. Overall, the reform equalized the terms on which employers anticipate leave use among men and women but job discrimination, which attaches to caregiving rather than anticipated leave, remains intact. </details>

**[González Motos, Sheila](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=RCn34NAAAAAJ&hl=en) & Marinova, Dani M. "The Division of Care after Equalizing Parental Leave Entitlements in Spain"**

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
Over decades, men’s time in childcare has increased while their relative share has stalled or even declined. Paternity leave is widely promoted as a policy instrument for involving fathers in childcare, yet it remains unclear whether it redistributes care responsibility to fathers beyond adding extra time. We draw on an original three-wave survey of parents with children born before and after an ambitious Spanish reform which extended non-transferable paternity leave and equalized it in length to maternity leave. Unlike earlier research, our survey instrument measures the relative distribution of tasks as well as cognitive labor. Longer entitlements were associated with a more equal division of physical care during the months fathers were at home, but the association weakened in the second trimester and disappeared by age one. Housework responded less and only during the leave itself, while the division of cognitive labor did not respond at all to the reform. Across five successive cohorts exposed to equal entitlements, no norms diffusion effects were observed. Overall, equal leave entitlements for fathers shifted the relative distribution of care only modestly and temporarily.
</details>

**Marinova, Dani M. "Can parental leave policies offset the political cost of motherhood? Cross-national and panel evidence from two decades of reform"**

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
Parenthood widens the gender gap in political engagement, and its impact endures across the life course. We test whether the duration, generosity and distribution of parental leave entitlements can offset the political cost of motherhood by shaping mothers’ attachment to the labor market, replacing their income and/or redistributing caregiving to fathers. Our identification strategy traces the impact of within-country variation in leave policy across birth cohorts, using ten rounds of the European Social Survey (2004–2023), corroborated with panel evidence testing a UK reform expanding maternity leave and a Spanish reform expanding father-specific leave on cohorts of parents exposed to each. Results show that only paid maternity leave boosts women’s political interest after childbirth, pointing to the importance of material resources in cushioning the economic strain of motherhood—an effect that outweighs any cost of mothers’ longer absence from work. In contrast, non-transferable leave for fathers shows no detectable effect. This is, to our knowledge, the first evidence of parental leave systems influencing the gender gap in political engagement.
 </details>

**Marinova, Dani M. "Involved but Not Responsible? Paternity Leave and the Division of Care."**

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
Paternity leave has become a key instrument of gender equality policy, yet evidence on its capacity to durably reshape the domestic division of care remains mixed. Complicating this picture, emerging evidence suggests that paternity leave may primarily enable fathers who already endorse shared-responsibility norms rather than fostering such norms more broadly. This review introduces a conceptual distinction between involvement—the execution of specific care tasks—and responsibility—accountability for care as an ongoing domain—to help explain these inconsistent findings. Existing studies consistently show that paternity leave can increase fathers’ participation in care tasks, particularly discretionary activities and in the short term. However, there is little evidence on whether it shifts the underlying distribution of responsibility within households. We identify two key gaps in the literature that are critical for assessing the impact of paternity leave on the distribution of care and for addressing persistent methodological challenges. First, cognitive labor—the anticipatory, planning, and monitoring work before and after task execution—provides a crucial empirical anchor for the concept of responsibility, yet has been largely overlooked in existing research. Second, solo paternity leave appears most likely to facilitate a transfer of responsibility, but the empirical evidence remains limited. Drawing on this review, we outline priority directions for future research and recommend methodological approaches to better address issues of self-selection.
</details>
